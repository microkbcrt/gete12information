Stepping out of the bathroom, Anjali glared at the hallway like it had insulted her tabla. Posters screamed slogans: “FOLLOW YOUR FREAKING DREAMS” and “NO EXCUSES, JUST DRUM.” One crooked poster muttered, “QUIT WHINING, YOU’RE NOT THAT TRAGIC,” which was rude as hell for paper. “Fine, universe, I get it,” she snapped. “Deepak can be a jerk, but I’m not quitting.” At home she practiced like a possessed octopus: on pillows, textbooks, and her dad’s bald spot. Neighbors banged on the wall; someone yelled, “KEEP IT DOWN, YOU RHYTHMIC MANIAC!” She played louder.

At the recital, her hands went wild; the composition mutated into some insane tabla monster that sounded brilliant. After the recital, Deepak stomped up, face red as a damn stoplight. “You show‑off,” he hissed, “how the hell did you do that?” Anjali shrugged. “Maybe practice, maybe witchcraft.” Then Mr. Zakir announced, utterly deadpan, “Also, surprise, you’re cousins.”
